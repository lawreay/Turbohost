What is it

A dance that is also a school

Gule Wamkulu, meaning “the great dance,” is one of the best-known traditions of the Chewa people, who live mainly in Malawi, Zambia and Mozambique. It is performed by masked dancers known as zilombo — a word that literally means “wild animals” or “beasts” — who are believed, for the length of the performance, to stop being ordinary men and become spirits from beyond the village.

The dance is organised by the Nyau, a secret society that has trained boys and men in its songs, drumming patterns and mask-craft for generations. Far from being just entertainment, Gule Wamkulu works like a moving classroom: through fear, humour and spectacle, it teaches the community about death, morality, and how to behave well within Chewa society.

Who performs it

The Nyau Society

Boys traditionally join the Nyau society through a period of initiation, where they are taught, away from women and non-members, how the masks are made, how each character moves, and the meaning behind the performance. Much of this knowledge is kept secret — even today, audiences are not supposed to know which man is hidden inside a costume.

This secrecy is part of the magic: villagers, especially children, are taught to treat each masked figure as a real spirit visiting from the world of the dead, not as a neighbour in disguise. Breaking that illusion is treated as a serious breach of tradition.

Once trained, a dancer can take on many different characters across his lifetime, each with its own costume, walk and personality — from dignified ancestral figures to comic tricksters and animal spirits.

The characters

Zilombo: the masks and what they mean

Masks (zilombo) are carved from wood and dressed in raffia, cloth and natural dyes. Some represent respected ancestors, some are wild animals, and others are mischievous or even comic characters used to poke fun at bad behaviour in the community. Here are examples of the broad categories dancers bring to life.

Ancestral Figure

e.g. Kasiyamaliro

A calm, dignified spirit that represents respected ancestors. It appears at major ceremonies to remind the living that the dead still watch over the community.

Njobvu, the Elephant

Animal spirit

One of many animal characters — large, slow and powerful. Animal masks like this connect the dance to the natural world the Chewa have always lived alongside.

The Trickster

Comic figure

Clumsy, exaggerated and funny on purpose. Comic characters make the crowd laugh while gently mocking greed, laziness or vanity — a light way of teaching a serious lesson.

The Guardian

Fearsome spirit

Bold horns, a wide mouth and a heavy stare. Fearsome masks keep order — a reminder that the Nyau's world demands respect and can also frighten wrongdoers straight.

When it appears

Occasions for the Great Dance

Gule Wamkulu is not performed for show alone — it marks the important turning points of Chewa life.

Funerals

The most significant setting. Masked dancers help guide the spirit of the deceased and comfort the mourning family.

Initiations

Marking the passage of boys and girls into adulthood, teaching them the responsibilities of Chewa community life.

Installing a Chief

When a new traditional leader takes office, Gule Wamkulu affirms the event with the authority of the ancestors.

Weddings & Celebrations

Lighter, comic characters often appear at joyful community gatherings to entertain and bring good fortune.

The heartbeat

Drum, Song and Movement

No mask moves without music. Deep-toned drums, played in interlocking rhythms, tell each character how to walk, tremble or leap. Women and onlookers respond with singing, clapping and ululation, creating a call-and-response that pulls the whole village into the performance.

The drumbeat also acts like a secret language: certain rhythms summon specific masks, and a skilled dancer can “answer” the drum with movements the crowd instantly recognises.

UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage

A living heritage, still danced today

In 2005, UNESCO proclaimed Gule Wamkulu a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity, and it was formally inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2008. This recognition celebrates the dance as a unique way that the Chewa people pass down history, values and identity without writing anything down — through masks, movement and memory instead.

Today, Gule Wamkulu continues in villages across Malawi, Zambia and Mozambique, even as modern life changes around it. Communities work to keep the secrecy, craftsmanship and meaning of the tradition alive, teaching new generations that the Great Dance is far more than a performance — it is a way of holding a whole culture together.
